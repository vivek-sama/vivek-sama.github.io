The challenge

The 2026 Vanguard ETF Trading Challenge, run by Vanguard and Temple University's Fox School of Business, drew 116 teams of four from 63 universities, including Duke, UPenn, UChicago, UC Berkeley, Dartmouth, Johns Hopkins, Michigan, Northwestern, UCLA and NYU. Each team managed $250,000 of simulated capital in Vanguard ETFs, with at most $62,500 in any one fund, limits on cash, and a minimum number of trades. Teams were ranked on Sharpe ratio, so the goal was the best return for each unit of risk, not the highest return.

The decision that mattered

We began with a mix of growth and dividend equity ETFs (VGT, VYM, VIG). Halfway through, we broke into the top 10. At that point, I decided our job was to protect a strong risk-adjusted position, not to take more risk.

I moved half the portfolio entirely into short-term bond and Treasury ETFs (VBIL, VUSB, VGUS, VGSH). The other half I split evenly between growth and defensive holdings, so about three-quarters of the portfolio was defensive.

When the market fell sharply, many teams lost their gains. Our portfolio held up, and we climbed from the top 10 into the top 5.

The final: pitching a new ETF to Vanguard

The top six teams then pitched a new ETF to judges from Vanguard. We proposed Vanguard BondBuilder TIPS: ten target-maturity TIPS ETFs, one for each year from 2027 to 2036, so a retiree can lock in the first ten years of spending in inflation-adjusted dollars.

I presented the product: how each fund works as one rung of a ladder, how it pays out in its final year, and how a retiree would hold it alongside stocks. Our backtest covered 166 ten-year retirements starting between 2003 and 2016, using the real yields available at each start date. Those are modeled results, since the funds don't exist yet.

Vanguard's judges ranked our pitch first of the six, which gave us 1st place overall. We'll visit the New York Stock Exchange for the closing bell on December 4.

What I learned

The metric you're judged on should shape every decision. Under a Sharpe ranking, cutting volatility was worth more than adding return, and the hardest call was choosing not to chase the rally.
