Anthropic’s IPO: Is the Growth Worth the Price?

Anthropic’s growth is real. The question is whether the business can earn enough to justify a $1.19 trillion market cap and $900.9 billion in commitments.

Anthropic raised approximately $93.5 billion in the largest IPO in history. The company behind Claude priced its shares at $83 on November 18, 2026, and began trading on Nasdaq the following day under the ticker ANT.

The offering implied a valuation of roughly $1.5 trillion on a fully diluted basis, although that figure included shares reserved for future equity awards.

What interests me isn’t necessarily how much Anthropic raised. It’s whether the underlying business can eventually justify that valuation.

The growth is real

Anthropic reported $33.24 billion in revenue for the first nine months of 2026, compared with $2.55 billion during the same period in 2025. By October 31, its annualized revenue run rate had reached approximately $81 billion.

Enterprise adoption has been especially strong. Customers generating at least $100,000 in trailing annual revenue increased from more than 950 in September 2025 to more than 11,900 a year later.

But I think it’s important to distinguish annualized revenue from actual reported revenue. The $81 billion figure extrapolates recent activity. It doesn’t necessarily represent what Anthropic will recognize over a full year, especially if customer spending changes.

The cost of growth

Anthropic’s financials also show how expensive it is to compete in AI.

Revenue is growing considerably faster than compute expenses, which is encouraging. However, the improvement hasn’t been consistent. Compute gross margin declined from 52% in Q2 2026 to 42% in Q3, partly because Anthropic relied on more expensive short-term capacity.

Profitability tells a similar story.

For the first nine months of 2026, Anthropic reported an operating loss of $1.26 billion and a net loss of $104.04 billion. The enormous difference largely reflects $94.32 billion in fair-value charges related to convertible notes and other financial instruments.

Those charges are non-cash and come from how earlier financing is valued, not from running the business. For evaluating the underlying business, I find the operating results more informative.

Even those results are volatile. Anthropic went from $974 million in operating income in Q2 to a $567 million operating loss in Q3. The company attributed the loss mainly to $5.0 billion of higher-cost short-term compute and a $2.0 billion non-cash donation-matching charge.

The $900 billion question

The most concerning figure in the prospectus, in my opinion, is Anthropic’s approximately $900.9 billion in purchase commitments and lease agreements, primarily related to computing infrastructure.

Of that amount, $451.6 billion is expected to be incurred before the end of 2030, including $70.7 billion within the 12 months following September 30, 2026.

For comparison, Anthropic estimated approximately $117 billion in combined net proceeds from its IPO and concurrent $26 billion private placement, including the full underwriters’ option exercise.

This doesn’t mean Anthropic faces an immediate funding shortfall. It already had substantial liquidity and expects to finance these commitments through revenue and additional capital.

Still, the scale of these obligations creates a real risk. Anthropic is committing to infrastructure capacity years before knowing precisely how much demand will exist.

If demand stays strong, that capacity could become a competitive advantage. If growth slows, the commitments could become a financial burden.

What does the valuation require?

Anthropic closed November 20 at $88.91 per share. With approximately 13.37 billion shares outstanding, its market capitalization was roughly $1.19 trillion.

Using its $81 billion annualized revenue run rate, that’s approximately 15 times sales.

But using reported trailing-12-month revenue of approximately $35.3 billion, the multiple is closer to 34 times sales.

The difference matters.

Here’s a simple valuation test using my own assumptions:

AssumptionValue
Market capitalization$1.19T
Target P/E multiple25×
Assumed net profit margin30%
Required annual net income$47.6B
Required annual revenue$159B

At a 25× earnings multiple and 30% net margin, Anthropic would need approximately $159 billion in annual revenue to support its current market capitalization.

That’s nearly twice its October revenue run rate.

And a 30% net margin is a demanding assumption for a company with substantial compute costs. At a 20% margin, required annual revenue increases to approximately $238 billion.

This isn’t a price target or full DCF valuation. It’s a simplified test of how much future profitability investors are pricing in.

The circular financing risk

Another detail worth watching is Anthropic’s relationship with its infrastructure partners.

Approximately 44% of its revenue during the first nine months of 2026 came through cloud service provider marketplaces.

Meanwhile, AMD, Broadcom, Google, Micron, Microsoft, Nvidia, and TSMC Partners participated in a $26 billion private placement.

These relationships aren’t inherently problematic. Cloud marketplaces distribute Anthropic’s products to real customers. But when suppliers, investors, and distribution partners overlap, I think it’s especially important to understand the economics behind reported growth.

There is also a revenue-quality consideration. Anthropic estimates that 5%–7% of its nine-month revenue came from customers violating its usage policies or commercial terms. Stricter enforcement could affect future growth.

What would change my mind

I’d become more optimistic if Anthropic consistently reduced compute expenses below 50% of revenue, returned to sustained operating profitability, and maintained strong customer growth without relying on increasingly expensive capacity.

Conversely, slowing revenue growth alongside deteriorating margins would make its current valuation much harder to justify.

Final thoughts

I think Anthropic has a credible path to becoming one of the most important enterprise AI companies. Its customer growth and improving long-term compute economics support that possibility.

But a great business isn’t automatically a great investment at every price.

For me, the biggest question isn’t whether demand for AI will continue growing. It’s whether Anthropic can capture enough of that growth, at attractive enough margins, to justify the capital it needs.

The IPO proves investors are willing to finance that future. It doesn’t prove the future will deliver the returns they’re expecting.
